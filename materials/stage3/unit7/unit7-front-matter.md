## The Campaign — حملة الوحدة
> **Your mission:** pack detail into one sentence instead of three — and keep your listener with you while you do it.

**This unit is about ranking information.** Deciding what is your point and what is context, then building a sentence
that shows the difference. It is the clearest single marker of a speaker who has stopped assembling sentences one idea
at a time.

**Half of it is pronunciation, and that is not a metaphor.** In writing you mark these clauses with commas. **In speech
there are no commas** — there is a pause, a pitch dip, and a return. A learner with perfect clause grammar and no
pauses has not learned this unit, because the prosody is what carries the meaning.

## The Lessons — دروس الوحدة

| # | Lesson | You'll be able to… |
|---|---|---|
| **L01** | Non-defining clauses | tuck extra information into the **middle** of a sentence and get out again |
| **L02** | Defining **or** non-defining | the comma that changes **how many sisters you have** |
| **L03** | Reduced relatives | *the man standing there* — the cheapest naturalness upgrade in the stage |
| **L04** | Prepositions + *which/whom*, and sentential *which* | *…, which meant we had to start again* |
| **L05** | Unit task 🎙️ | **2 minutes** of rich, layered description — and the pauses to match |

**Grammar spine:** non-defining clauses (*who/which*, no *that*, no omission, commas both sides) · the
defining/non-defining meaning distinction · reduced relatives, active *-ing* against passive *-ed* · preposition
placement across registers · *which* referring to a whole clause.
**Accent Lab:** the **spoken comma** — pause, pitch dip, return · **no break at all** inside a defining or reduced
clause · the pause before sentential *which* · participle endings surviving at speed · unstressed end-position
prepositions.
**Mindset:** adding detail without losing the listener — richness and clarity together.
**Arabic support:** **0%** — full immersion.

## The Prize — جايزة الوحدة
- The ability to say in **one** sentence what you used to say in three.
- ***…, which meant…*** — stating a fact and its significance in a single breath.
- Sentences that sound like an adult telling you something, rather than a form being filled in.

> [!TEACHER]
> **Unit overview:** 5 lessons (L02–L04 ~35 min, L01 and L05 ~40) plus this wrapper.
> **L01: the prosody is what makes this real, not the commas.** Learners can punctuate a non-defining clause on paper
> and produce it as an undifferentiated run in speech — which changes the meaning. Drill the three-part shape (pause,
> drop, return) and watch specifically for the **missing second pause**, which is the one that disappears. Unlike most
> of Stage 3 this lesson has genuine **rules**: no *that*, nothing omitted, commas both sides. State them as rules.
> Use the **delete test** as the definition rather than any explanation.
> **L02: teach it via meaning FIRST, punctuation second.** The only question is *does this clause tell me which one?*
> A learner who memorises comma rules without the meaning test will punctuate confidently and wrongly. Build everything
> on the pair — *my sister who lives in Dubai* against *my sister, who lives in Dubai* — and make the consequence
> concrete with the some/all example (*the employees who were consulted* = some; *the employees, who were consulted,*
> = all). Those are different facts in a report. The **already-unique noun** rule (names, *my mother*, *Cairo*) settles
> most real cases instantly. Watch for the **inserted courtesy pause** before defining clauses; it is a very common
> habit that silently changes every sentence.
> **L03 is the fastest naturalness upgrade in the stage** — no new grammar, two words deleted, and a markedly more
> fluent result. Say that, because the structure looks like a shortcut rather than a skill. The whole difficulty is the
> **active/passive choice**: ask what the noun is **doing**. Keep the scope tight — only defining clauses, only with
> *be* or a simple tense — or learners over-generalise. And spend time on the **reading** payoff: when a noun is
> followed by a participle the main verb comes later, and this is why long academic sentences collapse on learners who
> cannot see reductions.
> **L04: teach the end-position preposition as the DEFAULT.** *The person I spoke to* is normal, correct English.
> Learners told that *to whom* is "more correct" start producing it casually and sound absurd — a formal-register error
> is as visible as an informal one. Give two minutes to prepositions and five to **sentential *which***, which is the
> real content: it is how English attaches an evaluation to a whole event, and learners never produce it unprompted.
> Memorise the units — *which meant*, *which is why*, *which was fine*. Kill ***what*** on sight; it is a direct
> interference error.
> **L05: reward clarity and pacing, not clause-stuffing.** This is the unit where a learner can build impressively
> complex sentences nobody can follow. Round 4 of the rehearsal — take an over-stuffed sentence and split it into two,
> then decide which is better — is the antidote and the most valuable part of the lesson. Push item 6 hard: **detail
> beats adjectives**. *She was a great manager* is an adjective; *she gave me a project nobody wanted in my second
> week* does the same job better. Assess **pausing** as seriously as grammar.
