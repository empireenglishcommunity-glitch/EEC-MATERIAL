## The Campaign — حملة الوحدة
> **Your mission:** talk about the past that **didn't happen** — and about how the past you did have is still
> shaping today. Then turn one regret into a rule you actually follow.

**This is the hardest grammar in Stage 3, and it will feel wrong for a week.** Not because the pieces are new —
you already own the past perfect and *would* — but because these structures deliberately **refuse to match their
tenses**. That is the point of them, and the discomfort passes.

**Half of this unit is not grammar at all.** L02 is about how English criticises without starting an argument. L04
is about the discipline of naming a regret once and extracting something from it. Both are skills that happen to be
carried by conditional forms.

## The Lessons — دروس الوحدة

| # | Lesson | You'll be able to… |
|---|---|---|
| **L01** | Third conditional — form | *If I'd known, I'd have told you* — and the sound */dəv/* |
| **L02** | Third conditional — in use | **regret**, **gentle criticism**, **hypothesis** — one form, three jobs |
| **L03** | Mixed conditionals | past cause → **present** result: *I'd be there **now*** |
| **L04** | *wish* / *if only* | a **past regret** — and how it differs from a present wish by one syllable |
| **L05** | Unit task 🎙️ | **2 minutes** on a decision you'd change, ending in a rule you follow |

**Grammar spine:** *If* + past perfect → *would/could/might have* + participle · mixed conditionals (past condition →
present result, with the reverse for recognition) · *wish / if only* + past perfect against the B1 present wish ·
modal perfects for softened criticism.
**Accent Lab:** */dəv/* — the reduction that **is** this structure · the three near-identical openings */ɪfaɪd/*,
*/aɪdbi/*, */aɪdəv/* carrying three time frames · */aɪwɪʃaɪd/* as one run · stressed, slowed *if only* · **never
stress the subject** in a criticism.
**Mindset:** reframing regret as data — name it, take the lesson, let it go.
**Arabic support:** **0%** — full immersion.

## The Prize — جايزة الوحدة
- The ability to **complain without accusing** — the single most useful professional move in this stage.
- A **two-minute reflection** on a real decision that ends in something you now do differently.

> [!TEACHER]
> **Unit overview:** 5 lessons (L02 and L04 ~35 min, the rest ~40) plus this wrapper.
> **L01: the reduction is not polish, it is the lesson.** */aɪdəv/* is how this structure is said **and heard**, and a
> learner who only knows the full form cannot follow a native speaker using it at speed — which is the bigger loss.
> Do perception before production; most learners hear *nothing at all* in the *would have* slot the first time, and
> that discovery is the motivation for everything else. Three errors to name explicitly: ***would of*** (it is
> always *have*), the **double *would have*** (*"If I'd have known"* — the commonest overcorrection at this level),
> and a *would* in the *if*-clause. Call back to the */aɪləv/* against */aɪdəv/* drill from U3-L02.
> **L02 is the professionally valuable lesson.** The softening ladder — drop *should*, drop the **subject**, shift to
> ***we*** — is how English criticises without triggering a defence. Be explicit that ***should have* is not soft**:
> it carries obligation, so it always implies fault, and learners reach for it believing indirectness equals
> politeness. The subjectless form (*"it would have helped to know"*) is the one they will never produce unprompted
> and the one most worth drilling. And teach the **three tunes** — the identical sentence is an observation, a
> complaint or an accusation depending on stress. Without intonational control this grammar is a liability, because
> they will accuse someone while believing they were being diplomatic.
> **L03: teach ONE pattern properly.** Past condition → present result is what people actually say; the reverse is
> for recognition only. Overloading here makes learners avoid conditionals altogether. Give them the two-question
> self-check (*when was the cause? when is the effect?*) and the tell (**a present time word** — *now, still, by
> now* — in the result clause), which is far more usable than parsing clauses. **Reward meaning over form**, and say
> so out loud, or they will freeze.
> **L04: the contrast pair IS the lesson.** *I wish I knew* against *I wish I'd known* — one syllable, two decades.
> Drill it in perception; learners routinely discover the two have been sounding identical in their own speech.
> Hold the line on the mindset frame: **every regret must arrive with a lesson**, and refuse the ones that don't —
> kindly, but refuse them. Also flag *wish* used for something still achievable, which is how a regret becomes an
> excuse.
> **L05: do not expect flawless mixed conditionals.** This is the stage's hardest grammar produced under time
> pressure two lessons after introduction. Score **meaning and reduction**. Require the ending to be a **behaviour**
> — *"I sit on big decisions for a week"* — not a feeling. Let anyone choose a professional decision and accept a
> pass without comment.
