## 1. Our teaching philosophy in one paragraph

We do not teach *about* English; we build the *skill* of using English. Knowledge of rules is cheap and
already free everywhere. What learners lack is the ability to understand real English and produce it
under real conditions, consistently, without freezing. So every hour of EEC is engineered to (a) flood
the learner with understandable English, (b) force them to produce it, (c) correct them kindly and
precisely, (d) make it stick through spaced review, and (e) keep them showing up. Results come from that
loop repeated hundreds of times — not from any single clever trick.

---

## 2. Evidence base (why the method is shaped this way)

The method is built on well-established findings in second-language acquisition and learning science.
Summarized (and rephrased for compliance):

- **Comprehensible input** — learners acquire language by understanding messages slightly above their
  current level ("i+1"). Rich, meaningful input is the engine of acquisition, and input-heavy approaches
  can be far more efficient than pure grammar drilling.
- **Output is also required** — understanding is not enough. Being pushed to *produce* language, and
  getting feedback, is what forces learners to notice gaps and build accuracy and automaticity.
- **Interaction** — negotiating meaning with others (asking, clarifying, responding) accelerates
  acquisition beyond passive input alone.
- **Spacing & retrieval** — revisiting material across spaced intervals, and actively recalling it
  (testing yourself), produces far stronger long-term retention than massed cramming.
- **Affective filter** — anxiety, low motivation, and fear of embarrassment block acquisition; lowering
  them (safety, encouragement, Arabic scaffolding early) raises intake.
- **Deliberate practice** — targeted practice on specific weaknesses with immediate feedback drives
  skill gains; this is the basis of the Accent Lab.
- **Adult accent is trainable** — there is no hard biological cutoff; adults can reach highly intelligible,
  near-native pronunciation with focused, sustained practice — though a fully native accent is rare and
  never promised (see strategy GC-5).

*Content was rephrased for compliance with licensing restrictions.*

**Design consequence:** we refuse the false "input vs. output" and "grammar vs. immersion" debates. We
sequence input *and* output, use grammar *in service of* communication, and scaffold with Arabic early
then remove it.

## 3. The EEC Method — the six pillars

1. **Comprehensible Input (i+1)** — abundant, interesting, level-appropriate English.
2. **Pushed Output with Feedback** — required production, corrected precisely and kindly.
3. **Spaced Repetition & Retrieval** — nothing is taught once; everything resurfaces and is recalled.
4. **Arabic-First Scaffolding that Fades** — Arabic lowers the beginner's fear, then is withdrawn.
5. **The Accent Lab** — deliberate, contrastive American-pronunciation practice for Arabic speakers.
6. **Mindset, Habit & Accountability** — the layer that makes the other five actually get used.

Pillars 1–3 are *how language is acquired*. Pillar 4 is *how we lower the barrier*. Pillar 5 is *our
signature edge*. Pillar 6 is *how we beat dropout* — the real enemy.

## 4. The Learning Loop (the heart of the method)

Every lesson, and even every live activity, is a cycle:

```
   ┌──────────┐   ┌────────┐   ┌───────────┐   ┌────────────┐   ┌──────────┐
   │ 1. INPUT │ → │2.NOTICE│ → │3. PRODUCE │ → │4. FEEDBACK │ → │5. REVIEW │ ─┐
   └──────────┘   └────────┘   └───────────┘   └────────────┘   └──────────┘  │
        ▲                                                                       │
        └───────────────────────── spaced return ──────────────────────────────┘
```

### 4.1 Input — "understand a lot"
- **Goal:** the learner understands a rich message that contains a little new language.
- **Teaching moves:** use story, dialogue, or video; support meaning with images, gesture, and (early)
  Arabic; pre-teach only the few words needed to unlock comprehension; check understanding, not memory.
- **Rule of ~90%:** input should be roughly 90% understandable. Too easy = no growth; too hard = anxiety
  and shutdown.

### 4.2 Notice — "see the target"
- **Goal:** make the target sound/word/structure *salient* so the brain can register it.
- **Teaching moves:** highlight it, contrast it, ask "what did you notice?", give a short, plain
  explanation (in Arabic at low levels). Keep explanations brief — noticing, not lecturing.

### 4.3 Produce — "use it, out loud, early"
- **Goal:** the learner speaks/writes using the target. Speaking is prioritized.
- **Progression:** **controlled** (repeat, substitute) → **guided** (structured prompts) → **free**
  (real communication). Never end a lesson without free-ish output.
- **Rule:** the learner should talk more than the teacher. Aim for maximum learner talk-time.

### 4.4 Feedback — "correct precisely and kindly"
- See the full model in §11. Core idea: not everything is corrected; the *target* of the lesson and
  *communication-blocking* errors are. Feedback protects confidence while building accuracy.

### 4.5 Review — "make it stick"
- Every new item enters a **spaced-review queue** (§8) and returns in later warm-ups, tasks, and
  challenges — ideally via *retrieval* (learner recalls it) rather than mere re-exposure.

## 5. Skill weighting (and why)

Arabic learners are typically strongest in reading/grammar rules and weakest in *speaking* and
*listening* — and speaking is where confidence and real-world payoff live. So EEC front-loads the oral
skills.

| Level | Speaking | Listening | Reading | Writing |
|------|:--:|:--:|:--:|:--:|
| Pre-A1 / A1 | ●●● | ●●● | ● | ● |
| A2 | ●●● | ●●● | ●● | ●● |
| B1 | ●●● | ●●● | ●● | ●● |
| B2 | ●●● | ●●● | ●●● | ●●● |
| C1 / Exam | ●●● | ●●● | ●●● | ●●● |

**Grammar policy:** grammar is taught as a *tool for meaning*, always anchored to a communicative use
("we learn the past simple so you can tell someone what you did yesterday"), never as decontextualized
rules to memorize.

## 6. The Arabic-scaffold fade (a defining rule)

Arabic is a **scaffold** — it lowers the beginner's fear and speeds comprehension — but it is removed on
a schedule so the learner ends in full immersion. Overusing Arabic creates dependency; never using it
terrifies beginners. We do both, in sequence.

| Level | Approx. Arabic in explanations | Instructions & classroom language | Learner output |
|------|:--|:--|:--|
| Pre-A1 / A1 | ~70% (concepts explained in Arabic) | Mixed, mostly Arabic | English words/phrases |
| A2 | ~40% (Arabic for hard points only) | Mostly English | English sentences |
| B1 | ~15% (rare, for abstract nuance) | English only | English, extended |
| B2 | 0% (English-only immersion) | English only | English, fluent |
| C1 / Exam | 0% | English only | English, nuanced |

**Do:** use Arabic to *unlock understanding fast* and to reassure. **Don't:** translate everything word
for word, let learners answer in Arabic when they can attempt English, or use Arabic past its level.

## 7. Designing & selecting Input

**What makes input "optimal" (not just comprehensible):**
- **Comprehensible** (~90%), **interesting/relevant** (adult, real-life, career/travel/social themes),
  **abundant** (volume matters), and **slightly challenging** (i+1).

**Sources & formats:** short dialogues, real-life scenarios, stories, graded audio/video, and — at
higher levels — authentic media (podcasts, shows, interviews). Prefer American-English models for accent
consistency.

**Teacher moves for making hard input comprehensible:** pre-teach 3–5 key words, use visuals/gesture,
slow slightly (don't distort), paraphrase, ask yes/no and either/or checks before open questions.

**Anti-pattern:** dumping authentic native-speed content on beginners "for exposure." That's noise, not
input. Match the level.

## 8. The Spaced-Repetition & Retrieval engine

**Principle:** teach once, review many times, on widening intervals; and make review *active recall*.

- **Tagging:** every vocabulary item, structure, and Accent Lab sound is tagged when introduced.
- **Return schedule (default):** same lesson → next lesson → ~3 days → ~1 week → ~2–3 weeks → monthly.
  (Tune to tooling; even a simple weekly "recall warm-up" beats none.)
- **Retrieval over re-reading:** warm-ups ask learners to *produce* prior language ("tell me 3 things
  you did yesterday") rather than re-read notes. Low-stakes quizzing is encouraged (the testing effect).
- **Personalization:** a learner's *weak* items (missed in quizzes, mispronounced) resurface more often.
- **Where it lives:** warm-ups, weekly review tasks, the entry challenge, and community practice.

## 9. Accent-training pedagogy (how the Accent Lab is taught)

The full sound syllabus is a separate artifact (spec Task 1.4). This section defines the **pedagogy**:

**Core techniques:**
1. **Minimal pairs** — hear then produce contrasts (e.g., ship/sheep, pat/bat) to build the perception
   that must precede production.
2. **Shadowing** — learner speaks *along with / just behind* native audio, copying rhythm and melody.
3. **Record-and-compare** — learner records themselves, compares to a model, self-diagnoses.
4. **Targeted feedback** — coach names the *specific* articulation fix ("bring your top teeth to your
   lip for /v/").
5. **Spaced re-drilling** — a learner's weak sounds return via the review engine until stable.

**Sequence for each sound:** perceive (hear the difference) → understand (how the mouth makes it) →
produce in isolation → produce in words → produce in phrases/sentences → produce in free speech →
maintain via spaced review.

**Prosody matters most for "sounding natural":** word stress, sentence rhythm, linking, and reductions
(gonna/wanna) often affect intelligibility more than individual sounds — teach them explicitly, don't
leave them to chance.

**Honesty rule (GC-5):** we coach toward a clear, confident, neutral American accent. We celebrate
progress in intelligibility; we never shame a residual accent or promise its complete removal.

## 10. Teaching mindset, habit & accountability

This is curriculum, not a pep talk. It runs as a parallel thread alongside language lessons.

**What we teach (topics):** identity ("I am becoming an English speaker"), why consistency beats
intensity, habit design (tiny daily reps, habit-stacking, environment cues), handling the plateau and
the fear of speaking, and self-compassion after mistakes.

**How we teach it (methodology):**
- **Model it:** the coach demonstrates the disciplined behavior expected.
- **Micro-commitments:** learners set small, public, specific goals at kickoff and each week.
- **Make progress visible:** show CEFR gains, streaks, and before/after samples — evidence fuels motivation.
- **Normalize the dip:** pre-teach the plateau so it doesn't feel like failure when it arrives.
- **Community accountability first, gamification second:** peers and check-ins carry the motivation;
  streaks/points *support* but never *replace* it (over-gamification backfires).

**Re-engagement rule:** WHEN a learner is inactive for a set period, a warm automated nudge fires,
followed by a human check-in — the message is care, not guilt.

## 11. The Feedback & Error-Correction model

Precise, kind feedback is where accuracy is built and where confidence is protected. Getting this wrong
(over-correcting) is a top cause of learners going silent.

### 11.1 What to correct (priority order)
1. **The lesson's target** (always — that's the point of the lesson).
2. **Errors that block communication / cause misunderstanding.**
3. **High-frequency, fossilizing errors** (common Arabic-transfer patterns worth fixing early).
4. *Not* every minor slip — especially not during free/fluency speaking.

### 11.2 When to correct
- **Accuracy activities (controlled/guided):** correct promptly.
- **Fluency activities (free speaking):** do **not** interrupt; note errors and give **delayed feedback**
  after the task, so flow and confidence stay intact.

### 11.3 How to correct (techniques, gentlest first)
- **Elicitation / self-correction:** prompt the learner to fix it themselves ("try that again…?").
- **Recast:** naturally repeat their sentence correctly ("Yesterday I *went*…").
- **Clarification request:** "Sorry, when did you go?" — pushes a reformulation.
- **Explicit correction + brief rule:** only when needed, and briefly.
- **Peer feedback:** structured, with sentence frames, in pods.
- **Model comparison:** for pronunciation, compare to the recorded model.

### 11.4 Tone
Always sandwich correction in encouragement; correct the *language*, never the *person*; celebrate
attempts and risk-taking. A learner who feels safe speaks more, and more speaking = more learning.

## 12. The standard lesson template (expanded)

Every lesson follows this shape (times illustrative for a ~30–40 min lesson; scale as needed):

| # | Phase | Purpose | ~Time |
|--|--|--|--|
| 1 | **Warm-up / spaced recall** | Retrieve prior language (active recall) | 3–5 min |
| 2 | **Input** | Comprehensible message with i+1 target | 6–10 min |
| 3 | **Language focus (Notice)** | Make target salient; brief explanation (Arabic per level) | 4–6 min |
| 4 | **Accent Lab micro-drill** | Target sound/prosody, tagged for review | 3–5 min |
| 5 | **Guided output** | Controlled → guided practice with prompt correction | 5–8 min |
| 6 | **Free output** | Real communicative speaking task (delayed feedback) | 5–8 min |
| 7 | **Feedback & self-record** | Delayed correction + learner records a sample | 3–5 min |
| 8 | **Assignment + review queue** | Homework + what resurfaces later | 1–2 min |

**Design checklist for every lesson:**
- [ ] Has both input and pushed output.
- [ ] Speaking is prioritized; learner talk-time > teacher talk-time.
- [ ] Arabic use matches the level's fade rule.
- [ ] Includes an Accent Lab element.
- [ ] Adds items to the spaced-review queue.
- [ ] Ends with the learner having *produced* real language.

## 13. Facilitating live sessions

Live time is precious and expensive — spend it on what recordings can't do: **real interaction,
speaking practice, and feedback.** Do not lecture live; put explanations in recordings.

**Run-of-show (reference):**
1. **Warm welcome + recall warm-up** (get everyone speaking in the first 2 minutes).
2. **Micro-recap** of the week's target (brief).
3. **Interaction core:** pair/pod speaking tasks, role-plays, opinion exchanges — coach circulates.
4. **Spotlight & delayed feedback:** a few learners share; coach gives group-level correction on common
   errors (no naming/shaming).
5. **Accent moment:** one focused pronunciation drill.
6. **Close:** wins, next commitment, preview.

**Facilitation rules:**
- Maximize learner talk-time; minimize monologue.
- Use pods to give shy learners lower-stakes speaking.
- Correct common errors at the *group* level; correct individuals gently and sparingly live.
- Protect psychological safety — celebrate attempts loudly.
- **Mixed levels:** differentiate prompts (same task, tiered support) rather than teaching to the middle.

## 14. Handling common challenges

| Challenge | Response |
|---|---|
| **Silence / fear of speaking** | Lower stakes: pods, sentence frames, prep time, Arabic reassurance early; celebrate every attempt |
| **The intermediate plateau** | Pre-teach that it's normal; add richer input + more free output; show evidence of progress |
| **Over-reliance on Arabic** | Enforce the fade rules; require English attempts before Arabic; increase English classroom language |
| **Mixed abilities in a cohort** | Tiered prompts, peer pods pairing stronger/weaker, optional stretch tasks |
| **Motivation dip / going inactive** | Re-engagement nudge → human check-in; revisit their "why" and goal |
| **Perfectionism / fear of mistakes** | Reframe errors as data; delayed feedback during fluency; model self-correction |
| **Accent frustration** | Focus on intelligibility gains; celebrate progress; honest expectations (GC-5) |

## 15. The 10 EEC Teaching Commandments (the one-page soul)

1. **Build the skill, not the knowledge.** Use beats rules.
2. **They speak more than you talk.** Maximize learner talk-time.
3. **Understand a lot, then produce a lot.** Input and output, always both.
4. **~90% understandable.** Match the level; never drown beginners.
5. **Correct the target and the blockers — not everything.** Protect confidence.
6. **Never interrupt fluency.** Save correction for after the free task.
7. **Teach once, review forever.** Everything returns via spaced recall.
8. **Arabic is a bridge you burn behind you.** Scaffold early, immerse later.
9. **Consistency beats intensity.** The learner who shows up wins — help them show up.
10. **Honesty over hype.** Promise clear, confident English; prove it with results.

## 16. How the method connects to measurement

The method and the assessment (spec Task 1.6) are two sides of one coin:
- **Speaking-first teaching** → **speaking-weighted assessment** (the 5-dimension CEFR rubric).
- **Pushed output + self-record** → the **before/after speaking samples** that prove transformation.
- **Spaced retrieval** → **formative recall checks** that also serve as review.
- **Accent Lab** → the **pronunciation dimension** of the speaking rubric.
- **Mindset/accountability** → **completion rate**, the KPI that predicts results.

If learners are taught this way and measured this way, the numbers (completion %, CEFR gain, % hitting
B2) become the marketing — exactly as the strategy intends.

## 17. Glossary

- **i+1** — input just above current level.
- **Comprehensible input** — understandable messages that drive acquisition.
- **Pushed output** — required production that forces learners to stretch.
- **Recast** — repeating a learner's utterance correctly, naturally.
- **Elicitation** — prompting the learner to self-correct.
- **Retrieval practice** — recalling from memory (vs. re-reading); strengthens retention.
- **Spacing** — distributing review over widening intervals.
- **Shadowing** — speaking along with native audio to copy rhythm/melody.
- **Minimal pair** — two words differing by one sound (ship/sheep).
- **Affective filter** — the emotional barrier (anxiety/low motivation) that blocks intake.
- **Fade** — the planned withdrawal of Arabic scaffolding across levels.
